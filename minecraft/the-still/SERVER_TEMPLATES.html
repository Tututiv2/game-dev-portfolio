Template one — zombie-led

The Still

Everything worth having is in the dark, and the dark can hear you.

Eleven years after the Hush. The infected didn't lose their minds, they lost their eyes — every one of them, at once, in the same hour. What replaced sight was hearing so acute that a dropped pebble carries eighty blocks through fog.

What's left of humanity lives on rigs: bolted steel platforms suspended over a drowned, fog-choked continent, strung with sodium lamps that run on fuel you have to go down and dig up. Everything of value is beneath the fog. You descend, you mine, and every block you break is a bell rung in a room full of things listening for bells.

The Din

A per-player noise value. Breaking a block adds Din. Efficiency adds more. AoE enchants add it per block in the volume — one Oblivion swing is forty-five bells at once. Din decays only while you're standing still, at 4/second, which is agonisingly slow when something is walking toward you.

And your sell price scales with the Din you were at when the block broke. Blocks cracked open at SWARM are worth 2.2×. At CHOIR, 4×. So the correct play — the play every good player converges on — is to deliberately climb into the danger band and mine right on the edge of what you can survive, then run. The grind dial and the terror dial are the same dial. That is the entire server in one sentence.

Power Is Loud

Every upgrade in the game either makes more noise or makes less money — there is no clean power. Muffling gear (wool wraps, felt-lined picks, the Hush I–V enchant at −8% Din and −4% Fortune per level) buys you safety by taxing your yield directly. The best players on the server run zero muffling and simply outrun the horde, and everyone can tell who they are by how fast the ground shakes when they log in.

The horde — blind, and built entirely around sound

  • EarCommon, slow, staggers toward the last loud thing it heard. Trivial alone, arithmetic in a crowd.
  • CrierDoes not attack. Screams. Instantly adds +150 Din and pings your exact position to everything within 80 blocks. Kill it first, and kill it quietly, which is a genuinely interesting problem.
  • LatticeA knot of four fused bodies. Splits into four Ears when broken — and breaking it is loud.
  • The Long QuietSpawns only at CHOIR. It cannot perceive you at all while your Din is zero, so it waits. Standing perfectly motionless in the dark while it breathes three blocks away is the best twenty seconds this server has.
  • The BellWorld boss, cathedral-sized, embedded in the drowned city beneath the deepest shaft. It rings. When it rings, every player on the server gains Din.

Islands — your rig, and its perimeter

Your island is a platform bolted over the fog, in a visible grid alongside everyone else's. It has a perimeter: a ring of lamp-posts burning Pitch, which you mine. Perimeter lit means a mob-free, buildable, decorated home. Perimeter dark means the rig gets visited after nightfall.

Pitch burn scales with rig footprint, so expanding your island permanently raises your upkeep. Your flex costs you forever, which is the cleanest money sink in any of these three designs.

The social layer comes free from the geometry: rigs are physically adjacent, and a dark rig is visible from three rigs away. You can look out from your porch and see exactly who ran out of fuel this week. Nobody had to build a shame leaderboard — the skybox is the leaderboard.

Rig tiers run 16×16 up to 96×96, bought with Salt, which only comes off the drowned flats at Depth 14+.

Space — the endgame, and the reveal

The Hush was not a virus. It's a transmission, it never stopped, and it originates from a fixed point in the sky that does not move with the stars. The endgame is the Listening Post: a server-funded orbital tether built off the tallest rig, and Ascension means you climb it.

Above the fog there is no air, so there is no sound, so there is no Din and there are no infected. The ore up there is worth ten times anything below. And the entire game inverts: you mine in absolute silence, with no horde and no meter — except that every block you break, the signal answers you personally, by name, in chat.

The Bone Market

Teeth buy mutations — permanent player traits that each carry a real downside, so builds diverge instead of converging. Deaf: −40% Din generated, and −25% of your own mob-detection range, because you can't hear them either. Hollow Lungs: breathe the fog, lose two hearts permanently. Chorded: you can see nearby players' Din — and they can see yours.

What people buy

Perimeter lamp colours. Your rig's lights are visible to every neighbour across the fog, every night, forever — it is the single largest passive flex surface in any of these three designs, and it's purely cosmetic. Beyond that: rig banners, personal ambient soundscapes, and fog-parting particle auras. No perk touches Din, yield, or safety. Ever.

The one lawPower is loud. Nothing in this game makes you stronger without also making you easier to find.

Template two — space-led

Husk Belt

The belt is running out, and the dead are still on shift.

The mining consortium went dark ninety years ago and nobody came to collect the bodies. Nothing decays in vacuum, so the crews are all still there — suited, frozen, and eventually standing back up. You're a contract miner with a debt number and an oxygen tank, working a belt that is visibly, permanently being consumed.

The Husk

When you die anywhere in the belt, your body stays where it fell — and then it gets up. A Husk spawns at your death coordinates, wearing a cosmetic ghost of the loadout you died in, with your username floating over it in grey. It has HP scaled to your contract level. It patrols that rock. Anyone can kill it, including you.

Killing your own husk refunds 60% of what you dropped. Killing someone else's pays you 35%, pays them nothing, and sends them a single line of chat: "Someone put you down. — <name>"

Husks accumulate. A dangerous asteroid becomes visibly a graveyard of named ghosts, and new players learn which rocks are lethal by reading the nametags before they land. A well-known player's husk becomes a trophy other people hunt. Your entire zombie pillar is player-generated — you never have to design an encounter for it again.

The Belt Is Finite

Asteroids are real, placed, shared world objects with a finite ore budget. Work a rock long enough and it depletes, cracks, and calves — visibly breaks apart and is gone from the map. Permanently. There are roughly 400 rocks at any moment and /belt renders them coloured by remaining yield, so server-wide depletion is public information.

New rock arrives on the Stream: every six hours, three to nine fresh asteroids drift in from outside, composition rolled at arrival. Occasionally something extraordinary shows up, it gets announced, and everyone burns fuel to be there.

You claim rocks with Fuel. A claim decays after 48 hours unworked, and it is contestable — a rival can file a Dispute, which opens a ten-minute PvP window on that rock and nowhere else. The consequence is that the belt develops real geography and real politics. Rich veins are worth fighting for, exhausted regions become derelict slums, and the map redraws itself weekly without you touching a single config.

Oxygen Is The Clock

Outside a pressurised volume you're on tank. O₂ drains constantly, faster while mining, much faster while sprinting. Running out doesn't kill you — it greys you out: colour drains from the world, sound muffles to a heartbeat, then damage starts, then you're a husk. Every trip is a timed sortie with a decision at the halfway mark.

Tanks are the upgrade ladder that pickaxes are elsewhere: T1 at 90 seconds up to T12 at 22 minutes. Suit modules trade oxygen for yield directly, and the greed curve writes itself.

Islands — a berth you keep, a rock you lose

Your Berth

A hab module on the station ring. Small, pressurised, permanent, decorated, clearance-gated. This is where your stuff lives and where you log out. It is safe and it never changes, which is exactly the point — it's the only thing in the game that doesn't.

Your Claim

An actual asteroid you own, seal, pressurise and build on. It's your real island, and it will die — when the rock calves, everything you built is jettisoned to storage and the rock is deleted from the world. Veterans develop a ritual around working a dying claim's last shift. Impermanence is the feature.

Events & sub-games

  • The StreamThe six-hourly arrival. Announced 90 seconds ahead with a trajectory. First player to break a block on a fresh rock earns First Cut — a permanent line on that asteroid's plaque, readable by everyone who ever mines it, until the day it calves.
  • DerelictsDead consortium ships, procedurally restocked, crewed by NPC husks. No mining — pure extraction dungeons, and the best gear source in the game.
  • The FoundryOre → components → rig parts → hulls. A genuinely deep crafting tree standing in for the enchant ladder.
  • The Quiet AuditPeriodically the consortium's automated systems wake up and repossess. A random claim turns hostile and spawns drones; whoever clears it takes the rock, owner or not.

What people buy

Their own corpse. Husk skins, husk auras, the shade of the nametag, the pose it stands in. Players will pay real money to have a good-looking body that other people have to fight — and it is impossible to make that pay-to-win, because a fancier husk is not a stronger husk. Then: hull liveries, berth decor, suit lights, and the trail your ship leaves crossing the belt.

The one lawEverything runs out. Oxygen, fuel, claims, rocks. The only permanent things you own are your rating and your reputation.

Overall
Heavy. The concept is the strongest of the three; the world management is the price.
Cheap
Husks. Oxygen. Fuel. Claims. All of these are bookkeeping on data you already track.
Expensive
Four hundred live, stateful, destructible world objects with async chunk edits and a public map. Calving a rock while three players stand on it is a real engineering problem and you should design the answer before you write the feature.

Template three — island-led

Skyrot

Your rank is a number on the Y axis, and everyone can read it from the ground.

The world is a warm black sea with things swimming in it. The only land left is what floats. You begin on a rotting hunk of dirt six blocks above the waterline, and the first thing you do is look up — because the entire server is stacked above you in the same skybox, and you can see every single person who is further along than you are.

Altitude Is Rank

There is no rank sign. There's a lift. Ranking up physically raises your island, permanently, in the shared world — twenty-six rungs at roughly nine blocks each from Y=64, then Strata 1–30 pushing toward the ceiling, then Break Orbit.

You can stand on your porch and look straight up at the endgame hanging over you, and look down at everyone you've passed. The server needs no leaderboard, no scoreboard rank column and no prefix, because the sky is the leaderboard and it's rendered in the client for free.

The Rot Tide

Every night — twenty real minutes — the sea rises. Usually to Y=80. On a Black Tide, past Y=140. Anything below the line floods with drowned, and your island's soil begins converting to Rotflesh Stone, which is worthless and has to be cut out by hand. Low altitude isn't just poverty, it's structural damage on a nightly timer.

And the rot is the currency. Rotflesh smelts into Bile, and Bile is the only thing that buys altitude. You cannot rise unless you have been flooded.

So the optimal player deliberately builds a sacrificial low wing hanging beneath their island, lets it drown every night, and harvests it at dawn. The punishment is the fuel. Players in the upper Strata have no rot at all, which means they must buy Bile from the poor — or lower anchors, chunks of their own island dropped into the tide on chains. An anchor rots, produces Bile, and can be cut by anyone who climbs it. A rich player's supply line is a physical, visible, severable object hanging in the dark.

Grafting

Islands grow by fusing land. Grafts are themed chunks — Salt Shelf, Coral Bone, Basalt Wedge, Glassed Plain, Bonefield, twenty-odd more — each with its own ore table, its own build palette, and its own rot resistance. They're pulled from the tide, dropped by tide bosses, auctioned, and won.

Your island is therefore a walkable, physical trophy case of everything you have ever earned. Two players at identical altitude look nothing alike. Prestige is calving: you snap the island down to a single graft of your choosing, fall back to Y=64, and keep both a permanent multiplier and your /atlas — the complete history of every graft you've held, each re-earnable at a 40% discount.

The drowned

  • ClingGrabs your island's edge and hauls itself up. Cheap, constant, and the reason you build a lip.
  • BloomA drifting spore mass. Doesn't attack — touches your island and rots a 7×7 of it instantly. You shoot these or you lose ground.
  • KelpwrightDrags players down, off the island, into the sea. There is no fall damage in this game because falling isn't the danger. Landing in the tide is.
  • Mother SaltBlack Tide boss. Walks on the surface of the water at the height of a twenty-block tower, and everyone on the server watches her go past.

Space — the thin band, and the ring

Past Strata 25 the atmosphere gives out. Above Y=280 there's no weather, no tide, and no air — the final band of islands sits in near-vacuum, and the ore that grows on the undersides of the highest rocks is skyrot itself: the origin of the infection, which has been falling down onto the world this whole time.

Break Orbit and your island detaches from the sky entirely and joins a slow orbital ring — visible to every player on the server, every night, as moving lights crossing overhead. New players see them on their first evening and ask what they are.

What people buy

The light in the sky. Break Orbit players are visible to the entire server every single night, and the colour and shape of that light is purchasable. Nothing else in any of these three designs is seen by that many people that often. Below that: graft skins, chain and tether styles, lift animations, and the pattern the tide leaves on your island's underside at dawn.

The one lawThe thing that hurts you is the thing that pays you. Rot, tide and altitude are one system, not three.

If you're picking one

Which of these to build

All three reuse your prestige maths, your pity system, your crate and enchant ladders, your config-GUI discipline and your cosmetics-only monetisation law. What differs is the central tension — and how much new engineering it demands before anyone can play it.

Build The Still if…

You want something playable in weeks, not seasons. It's one number layered over the game you've already written, and that number turns every existing enchant into a decision. Lowest risk, highest immediate return.

Build Husk Belt if…

You want the idea people will talk about. Player deaths generating the enemies is the best mechanic in this document and it costs almost nothing. Ship the husks alone on your existing server as a test before committing to the finite belt.

Build Skyrot if…

You want the one nobody has made. It's the most beautiful and the most dangerous — the whole design rests on one technical operation, so build that operation first and let it tell you whether the server is possible.
