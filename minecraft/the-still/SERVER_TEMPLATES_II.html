Three More Ways
To Be Found

The Din worked because of one rule underneath it: the infected perceive you through exactly one channel, and that channel is also your economy. Get that right and every enchant, every upgrade and every greedy decision retunes itself for free.

So here are three more channels. Not three reskins of sound — each one produces a genuinely different verb at the pickaxe, a different social shape, and a different endgame inversion.

Channel one — sight

Nightside

They stop when you look at them. You have one face, and the ore is behind you.

A tidally locked world around a red dwarf that is most of the way through dying. One face has been in daylight since the planet formed; one has never seen the star at all. The dayside is stripped — three generations of people too frightened to cross the line have taken everything there was. Everything that's left is on the nightside.

The nightside is full of statues. Some of them are statues.

You Cannot Mine And Watch

The Effigies freeze the instant they enter a player's gaze cone — 42°, forty blocks, line of sight required — and they hold the pose indefinitely. They have had centuries to get good at it, and the nightside is genuinely littered with real statuary they can stand among. The moment they leave your cone they move, and they move fast.

And mining requires facing the block. Every swing turns your back on the room. That single collision is the whole server: the resource you're spending isn't stamina or durability, it's attention, and you have exactly one unit of it.

Frozen — inside the cone
Free — everywhere else

Three held. Six moving. The faint outer wedge is Wide Angle at the top of the Watch tree — 78°, and still not a room.

42° base · 40 blocks · line of sight required · freeze is absolute and lasts as long as you hold it

Gaze Pauses. Light Petrifies.

Two different things stop an Effigy, and keeping them separate is what makes the rest of the server work.

Gaze pauses. Your look cone holds them perfectly, for exactly as long as you hold it, and the instant you look away they move. It's free, it's instant, it's unlimited — and it produces nothing. Gaze is how you survive a room.

Light petrifies. Anything held in real light long enough stops being able to move ever again. It becomes stone, permanently, and stone can be broken, and broken effigy is Chalk. Light is how you clear a room.

So a Watchman keeps you alive tonight and a lamp makes the shaft permanently safer. That split is why the terminator matters, why the Long Look is the Watch capstone, and — as it turns out — why anyone would ever point a lamp at somebody else.

The Watch

Because one player physically cannot mine and hold a room, the core loop is a pair: a Miner and a Watchman. Solo is entirely playable at roughly half speed, with a lot of turning around, and it is much more frightening — but the economy is built for two.

So the Watch gets its own full progression ladder. A support class in a prison server, which does not exist anywhere. Watch XP accrues per second of effigy-time held, and the perks are all about the geometry of attention rather than damage or yield.

The Terminator Moves

The lock isn't perfect — the planet librates, and the light line sweeps across the surface on a four-hour real cycle. Safe and lethal are separated by a moving frontier, and the richest ore sits just inside the dark, so the best shifts are worked in the sweeping shadow with daylight at your back as an escape route you can physically see.

Effigies caught by the advancing light are fixed permanently — they become real stone, and they can be broken for Chalk. When the line sweeps back, every unfixed statue in the shaft you just cleared stands up. Dawn is when mining gets easy. Dusk is when you find out how many of the things you'd been standing next to were waiting.

Islands — the lamp economy

Revised from your noteYou were right: the first draft had you aiming at a neighbour out of pure goodwill, which means nobody would ever do it. The fix falls straight out of the two-layer rule — light petrifies, and whoever's light did the petrifying owns the stone.

Why you'd point it at someone

A beam aimed at a working shaft fixes Effigies into stone all night long. That stone is Chalk, and it belongs to the lamp, not to the ground. The miner takes the ore. The lamp holder takes the Chalk. Two resources, one operation, nothing to split and nothing to argue about.

And aiming at your own quiet island petrifies nothing, because nothing comes to a quiet island. A lamp only earns when it's pointed at danger — which means pointed at somebody who is being loud, deep and productive.

What that turns into

Beam slots are limited per island by tier, so good positions are scarce and tradeable. A productive miner advertises — "L19, running till dawn, two slots open" — and lamp holders compete over the best shafts the way people compete over good real estate.

Your "I shine you, you shine me" handshake still happens, and now it happens because it pays both ways rather than as a favour. And swinging your lamp away mid-shift doesn't only kill your partner — it cuts off your own Chalk mid-flow. Betrayal costs money here, which is exactly why it stays rare, deliberate, and worth talking about for months afterwards.

Three careers, and none of them overlap The Miner takes the ore and takes the risk. The Watchman stands in the room and holds it in real time, for a cut and a public reputation. The Lamplighter never enters the shaft at all — they burn their own Lumen from kilometres away to farm Chalk off somebody else's danger. A prison server with a genuine third career that is neither combat nor mining does not currently exist anywhere.

Space — the Far Face

Revised from your noteAgreed — "your lamp replaces your eyes" was a weak ending. Same verb, worse tool, no reason to care. This is the version that earns the ascension.

The antipode is the point where light has never fallen. Not once, in four billion years. Nothing there was ever observed, so nothing there ever learned to freeze.

They learned to do it to you.

On the Far Face, being inside something's line of sight locks you in place. A Regard does not chase. It looks. You stop. Then it walks toward you without hurrying, and you watch it come the entire way. The polarity of the whole game reverses: you spent a career aiming your attention, and now the game is about staying out of somebody else's. Cover matters. Corners matter. Mining blind matters. The open ground you sprinted across carelessly for a year is now the thing that kills you.

And your Watch levels are the one thing that carries — a career spent holding eye contact is precisely what shortens their hold on you. The best Watchmen make the best survivors. It's the only point in the design where a support career converts into personal power, and it lands as a payoff rather than a handout because you spent a year earning it first.

The Audience is the world boss, and it isn't one thing. It's a ring of them, facing inward. Walk into the middle and you do not move again.

Channel two — heat

Rimefall

They don't want to eat you. They want to hold you.

An ice moon around a gas giant that takes up half the sky. The moon is flexed and kneaded by that gravity, which is why there's ore, why there are quakes, and why the ocean under the ice is warm. Something down there is the reason nothing on the surface is alive any more.

The survivors live in domes on the sheet and dig for what the flexing pushes up.

Core Temp Is A Band, Not A Bar

One number. Three forces pulling it in different directions, all of them lethal:

Heat keeps you alive. Below 28°C you are dying, visibly, on a clock.
Heat powers your tools. Every swing spends it — better picks spend more.
Heat is how they find you. Above 35°C the Cold can read you from ninety blocks.

The survivable-and-hidden window is about 31–34°C, and every system in the game is pushing you out of it. Veterans "run cold" — deliberately hypothermic at three hearts, invisible, one bad tick from dead. It'll be server slang inside a week.

The Material Sets The Band

In The Still you push a meter into the red and get paid for nerve. Here you are holding a needle inside a narrow window while the rock you're cutting decides where that window is. Rime crystal shatters if you're warm, so you work it while dying. Magmite won't cut if you're cool, so you work it while visible. Flexstone is fine anywhere, which is why it's cheap.

That single rule turns the entire gear tree into a thermal management vocabulary that no Minecraft server has ever used: vents, sinks, coolant loops, insulation, bleed valves. The enchant ladder becomes Bleed (dump heat fast), Kindle, Lag (slows change in both directions — the defensive pick), Deadman (auto-vents at 36°C, saves your life, wrecks your rime yield).

And it retunes everything you've already built for free, because every existing enchant just gains a heatPerProc field.

Your Dome Melts Its Own Foundation

Islands are hab domes standing on an ice sheet with a real, tracked, per-chunk thickness. Your dome's heat thins the ice under it. Your build's mass presses down on it. Both numbers are visible in your own base, on a panel you will learn to check obsessively.

Get it wrong and the dome goes through. Not a debuff — the whole thing, into the black water, gone. Thickness recovers slowly, so upkeep means pumping cold, reinforcing with Ballast, or simply building smaller than you can afford. Every other island sink in these six designs bleeds you gradually. This one waits, and then takes everything at once.

Space — the Warm Dark

The endgame is not up. It's down, through the sheet, into the ocean.

Down there the water is a constant 4°C and you cannot regulate at all — the band system you spent a year mastering simply stops functioning. You carry heat down in a tank, it only ever goes one way, and the tension changes shape entirely: not a needle to hold, a countdown to spend. Ascension doesn't scale the challenge. It replaces it.

And the thing the Cold were made from is down there, and it is warm, and it has been warm this whole time.

Channel three — scent, and memory

Greendark

It doesn't hunt players. It hunts wealth — and it never forgets where it found some.

The spores came down eleven years ago on something that fell out of orbit. What grew out of them is one organism, it covers the world now, and it is extremely patient. People live up in the canopy because the floor belongs to the Root.

You Smell Like What You Are Carrying

Your scent radius is a direct function of your inventory's sell value. Empty pockets are almost nothing. A full load is a flare. The Root does not track players — it tracks value, and it will walk past a broke man to reach a rich one.

Which means the grind loop and the horror loop are the same loop. Every prison server has a "mine until full, then walk to the sell chest" rhythm. This one makes that walk the actual game. Banking often costs you time. Banking rarely costs you everything.

And the counter-play writes itself without you designing any of it: decoys (drop a stack of gold to bloom a false plume and leave the other way — wealth as a smoke grenade), mules (split a haul so neither of you crosses a threshold), scent-tight cases (heavy, slow, few slots, and the only encumbrance system players will ever thank you for), and ambush — because /nose shows other players nearby plumes by intensity but never by identity. That ninety-metre bloom is either a rich stranger or bait, and there is no way to know but to go and look.

The Root Remembers, Server-Wide

Every infected on the map is a fruiting body of one mycelial network. What one learns, all of them know — permanently, decaying only very slowly. Killing them does nothing. The knowledge is in the floor.

The Root keeps a live heat map of where players actually went. Not a spawn table you wrote: a record of behaviour. Attention accumulates on high-traffic tiles. Attention becomes pressure. Pressure becomes ambush density, then root-walls, then a Bloom — a permanent hostile installation grown in the exact place your players love most.

This is a difficulty curve driven entirely by player behaviour, and it solves a real problem you already have. On every prison server everyone converges on the current meta mine and it goes stale and you nerf it in a patch. Here, the meta mine becomes the dangerous mine automatically. The map rebalances itself, popularity is punished by the world instead of by you, and you never write another nerf note.

The Creep

Attention isn't only a number in a database — the Root grows tissue into the places it's thinking about. Pale ropes, bladders, fans of something between fungus and muscle, spreading over ground, walls, ceilings and your own bridges.

It isn't a mob. It doesn't attack. It only has to be touched. Brush creep and it ruptures: a spore burst that spikes your scent radius by +60m for ninety seconds. You take no damage whatsoever. You are simply announced, in the worst place, at the worst possible time.

Creep grows fastest where attention is highest, so the routes everybody uses are the routes that slowly close. Traversing a popular corridor turns into a genuine platforming problem that gets tighter every week the server keeps using it — and you can read the server's whole history off the ground. Dense creep means this was somebody's favourite way home. New players learn where the money is by following the overgrowth, which is precisely where it is most dangerous.

The Boss Is Made Of Your Playtime

You were right that a Bloom shouldn't just sit there being a debuff. It should grow, it should be killable, and it should pay.

Past the creep threshold a Bloom takes root, and then it keeps feeding on the attention still flowing in. Seven stages, each bigger, meaner, wider — and worth more. Which means a Bloom's stage is a direct readout of how many hours your server collectively poured into that spot. Nobody spawns it. Nobody schedules it. You farm it into existence without meaning to, and then you have to deal with what you made.

And killing a Bloom wipes the attention inside its radius. The raid is the reset. Which closes the entire loop with nothing scheduled and nothing configured: grind → attention → creep → Bloom → raid → clean ground → grind.

People Will Farm Their Own Boss

Watch what falls out of that reward curve. Kill it early: easy, safe, cheap. Let it grow: your best mine gets harder every single day, and the payout climbs.

So gangs will deliberately feed a Bloom — keep grinding beside it to fatten it up, then defend it from other players who want it dead now because it's strangling their grove. One gang's investment is another gang's infestation, and both of them are right.

Defending a boss from being killed is a PvP objective no server has, because bosses are normally server property. Here the boss is somebody's savings account, and everyone can watch it getting bigger from the Bole.

Burning Back

Fire is the one thing the Root cannot remember through. A burned tile loses its accumulated attention and starts again at nothing.

So the server develops a real, communal, ritualised practice of burning back the places it works — and burning is expensive, slow, dangerous and visible from every platform in sight. The great mines stay great only because somebody maintains them, and maintenance is a public act that everybody benefits from and somebody has to pay for.

Gangs will form around burn schedules. Groves will be defended, taxed, and fought over on the basis of who cleared them last. That is your entire social endgame, and it grew out of one decaying float per chunk.

Spawn — the Bole

Your idea, takenOne enormous tree that everyone lives on, beats scattered canopy platforms outright. It gives the server a single silhouette, a vertical social ladder, and a descent into fog. Here it is with the one twist I'd add.

The Bole is a tree the size of a mountain, and the entire server lives lashed to its trunk — platforms, walkways, hollowed chambers, market halls cut into heartwood. Higher up the Bole means better districts, so where you live is legible to everyone without it being your rank.

And the twist: the Bole is part of the Root, and everybody knows it. It has never eaten anyone and nobody can say why. You live inside the mouth of the thing that is hunting you, under a truce no one understands, and there is an entire civic religion built around not giving it a reason.

You leave by the Long Ladder and descend the trunk into fog. You lose sight of the Bole about two thirds of the way down and everything below that is the game.

Bridges out on the floor are player-built and shared, and a bridge is a scent corridor — a rich neighbourhood drags pressure onto everyone connected to it, so who you let build toward you is a real economic decision. Wealthy districts fund burn crews or they get eaten. Poor districts stay quiet and stay safe. The class dynamics are entirely emergent and completely legible from the trunk.

Space — the Canopy Break

The thing that fell was a seedship, and most of it is still in orbit, and the planet is being terraformed into a launcher. The whole biosphere is one organ building a gun and aiming it at the next world.

Ascension is the Canopy Break — up past the treeline into the launch structure, where there is no soil, so no network, so no memory. Nothing up there has ever seen a human and nothing up there can ever learn.

Which sounds like relief for about a day. Then it lands: nothing you do up there accumulates. No place ever gets safer. No route ever becomes routine. Every encounter is first contact, forever, and you cannot make anywhere yours. Down below, the world knew you far too well. Up here it can never know you at all, and it turns out that's the worse of the two.

Currencies

  • MoneySells and gear
  • AshFrom burned tiles. Buys burn tools — spend the forest to fight the forest
  • SapGreat trees only. Canopy expansion
  • SporeHarvested from Blooms. Reeks
  • SterileCanopy Break. The only currency with no smell

Spore is the sharpest object in these six designs: a high-tier currency that increases your scent radius while you hold it. A currency you are punished for hoarding. Every prison server on earth has an inflation problem caused by players sitting on enormous piles of the top currency — this one makes the pile itself hunt you, and the economy self-drains without a single tax.

What people buy

Their own plume. Scent is rendered as a drifting particle trail whose density is your carried value, and its colour, shape and drift pattern are cosmetic. You can never make it smaller — only prettier. Rich players are ostentatiously, dangerously visible and they pay for the privilege of being visible in style, which is the single most pay-to-win-proof monetisation in this document. Below that: hold silhouettes, lantern moths, and bridge styles.

The one lawThe world keeps score. Everything you take, you carry — and everywhere you go, it learns.

Build cost

Overall
Medium — and the best idea-to-effort ratio of all six.
Cheap
Scent radius (a curve over a value you already compute). The heat map (a float per chunk with decay). The Flowering. The Deep. Spore.
Expensive
Burn mechanics and regrowth, and the canopy world itself — trees at terrain scale are a builder problem more than a code one.
Risk
An emergent difficulty curve can run away and quietly strangle your best mine. Cap per-tile pressure, decay it faster than you think you need to, and put the Root's heat map on a /config page from day one — you need to be able to see the map the way the Root sees it or you will be debugging a ghost.

Grafting — if you'd rather not start over

Three things you could steal for The Still

You're already building The Still, and these are whole servers. But each has one piece that bolts onto the Din without touching its design — and picking pieces beats picking servers.

Take the Watch

A support skill tree with a public reputation number, on machinery you already have. In The Still it becomes a Listener — a second player who stays silent so the miner can be loud, and whose Din budget is spent on someone else's greed. Turns a solo horror server into a two-person one for almost nothing.

Take Calor

A currency that is also your lifespan. In The Still the direct equivalent is bottled quiet — a consumable that eats Din, tradeable, giftable, stealable. Suddenly a rich player can buy their way out of a Choir and a poor one can sell them the chance to.

Take the heat map

One decaying float per chunk recording where players actually went — and it is channel-agnostic. It feeds noise, heat or scent identically, so it drops into The Still unchanged: the shafts everyone works get louder, ambient Din floor rising with traffic. Your meta mine nerfs itself forever and you never write another patch note. And the Bloom comes free with it — let a shaft's floor climb long enough and something should be living in it, worth killing, worth arguing over.
