The Still · fused build · noise + heat · both factions

Noise Calls.
Heat Keeps.

Din is a moment — it spikes, it brings them, it's gone in seconds. Heat is a place — it soaks into the room you worked, and it's why they don't leave when you go quiet.

You wanted both ideas and you were right to, because they turn out to be the same idea. Heat has to go somewhere. Whether it stays on your body or ends up in the room is the only real decision in the game, and it decides which monster finds you and whether this shaft still exists next week.

Sound is why they came. Warmth is why they stayed.

The Listening arrive on noise, fight, and disperse — they are an event. The Wanting arrive on warmth and move in. One is weather. The other is a tenant. And what you vent out of your suit is what invites the tenant.

Vent your heat and it doesn't vanish. It goes into the room.

Muffling gear is insulation — wool wraps, felt linings, sealed joints all trap heat by exactly the same physics that eats sound. Venting means opening up, and opening up is loud. That's the old tension, and it's real.

But here's the part that makes the whole thing one system instead of two: the heat you dump has to land somewhere, and it lands in the shaft you're standing in.

Run open and you're cold, invisible to the Wanting, loud as hell — and you are cooking your own mine at double rate. Run sealed and you're silent, personally blazing, hunted — and the shaft stays cold and workable for tomorrow.

So the dial isn't "which predator." It's your safety tonight versus this ground's future, and in a crew it becomes a conversation: somebody has to run sealed, or everybody vents and the shaft is dead by morning.

Two factions, and they are not two flavours of the same thing

The Hush blinded everyone at once, and what happened next depended on where they were standing. Near the rigs, the ones that sharpened their hearing ate. Deeper down in the cold, hearing found nothing — so the ones still moving are the ones that learned to want warmth.

They don't just hunt differently. They exist on different clocks, which is the entire justification for having two.

Heat stages — what a room becomes

Every block broken deposits heat into its region, scaled by your seal. It bleeds off at about −120/hour real time, so a hard session is workable again next evening and a properly cooked shaft takes most of a day.

You can see it from outside A warm room in a cold, drowned, fog-choked world steams. Heat state is rendered: haze at Warm, plumes at Hot, a shaft mouth breathing white at Warren. Stand at the end of a corridor and you can read this level's entire recent history off which doorways are smoking — including whether the crew ahead of you ran sealed or vented everything and left.

What this does to the map

Good spots burn out

Work a shaft hard and you cook it — not as a penalty applied to you, but as a fact about the room that's equally true for everyone who comes down next.

So veterans keep a rotation, the way you'd rotate fields. Four or five shafts on a cycle, working one while the others cool. Nobody has to be taught that; it's just what the numbers make correct.

Cold shafts are information

Untouched ground at depth is a real score — worth keeping quiet about, worth lying about, worth selling. And the fastest way to destroy a rival's rotation is to go and vent in it.

The commons problem

Here's what the seal really creates. Running open is individually correct — you're cold, you're safe from the Wanting, you outrun the Listening. And it wrecks the shaft for everyone including tomorrow's you.

In a crew, "who's running sealed tonight" becomes a genuine conversation with a genuine cost, and the person who takes it is doing the same job as a Watchman: personal risk, group benefit. Gangs will develop etiquette about venting, and breaking it will get people kicked.

And the ballsy play pays

A Warren is abandoned by everyone with sense, paying ×2.5 a block, at a depth you already unlocked, with zero competition. The top earners on this server will be crews who go and work rooms that were given up on — which is a completely different skill from a solo Din run, and it means The Still has an endgame activity that isn't just deeper.

The rock decides which one you fight

Deep ore doesn't only pay differently — it forces a seal position, and therefore forces a predator and a consequence for the shaft. You don't pick your lane freely. You pick what you're mining and the lane comes attached.

Cooling a shaft on purpose

The continent is drowned. There is water behind almost every wall down there and everyone knows it.

Breach the wall and the shaft floods. Heat goes to zero, the nest drowns, the room is clean — and every block of ore you hadn't taken is gone under water with it. An emergency button with a real price, entirely diegetic, and the source of the ugliest social problem on the server: flooding a Warren somebody else has been farming is not stealing, is not griefing under any rule you could actually write, and will start wars.

The Warmth is now load-bearing Your favourite mob — the thing that reads on thermal exactly like a rig lamp — is a heat source. It holds Wanting around itself and slowly cooks whatever room it stands in. A Warmth that spawns in your rotation is actively poisoning that shaft, and you have to go deal with it or lose the ground. It lures you in and it takes your best mine. That is a perfect mob and you invented its job by accident.

The Wake — griefing as content, not a ban

You spotted the hole: what stops somebody breaching walls at random all night, drowning ground they don't use, permanently degrading the server for no reason? Normally that's a ban. But a ban is a moderator doing a job the game should be doing, and it obeys your own law — disarm destructive capabilities in code, not with a warning in a doc.

So: flooding stays legal. It just becomes impossible to do anonymously, and expensive to do repeatedly.

LayerWhat it does
Breaching Charge You cannot breach bare-handed. Charges are craftable, genuinely expensive, and consumed. A vandal doing ten shafts a night has to fund ten charges, which kills most casual vandalism before any social system is involved.
Every breach is signed /flooded is a permanent public log — who, when, which shaft, how much heat was destroyed, how much ore went under. Forever. There is no anonymous flooding.
The Wake Each breach adds 6 hours of Wake, stacking, ticking only while you're online — so you serve it in public, you can't sleep it off. Wake I through V, visible above your head, in tab and on the scoreboard, deepening every level.
Loss of sanctuary A player with Wake is PvP-flagged everywhere — the shafts, the rigs, spawn, all of it. That's the punishment: not removal, exile from safety, in public, for a duration they chose.
The server co-signs Bounties normally cost the placer money. On a Wake player the server matches the bounty, scaling with Wake level. A serial flooder becomes a walking payday that the community funds together and the world pays out on.
Automatic restitution When they're killed, the bounty splits — the killer takes the largest share, and the rest is distributed to every player who had heat in the shafts they drowned. Victims are paid back without anyone filing anything.
The way out /restitution — pay a set price per flooded shaft directly to the people affected and clear your Wake early. Grief, get hunted, pay it back, done. No moderator ever touches it.
The Drowned Watch Wake VIII and above — sustained, funded, deliberate vandalism. The world starts hunting you personally: named hostiles that spawn on you, anywhere, including your own rig, and do not stop until the Wake clears. It is the in-fiction version of a ban and it is enormously more fun than a ban.
What this sorts out on its own Flooding your own cooked shaft: one Wake, six hours, mild risk, completely fine. Drowning a rival's Warren mid-war: you knew you'd be hunted and you did it anyway — that's a war move and it's supposed to cost. Random vandalism: you accumulate Wake faster than it decays, the whole server can read your log, you're bountied with the house matching, you have nowhere safe to stand, and eventually the water itself comes for you. Nobody has to be banned and the hunt is better content than the grief was.
Where PvP is live Rigs and D1–D9 are safe ground, so new players are never anyone's prey. D10 and below — the Crossing onward — PvP is on. Warrens are always on, no exceptions, because the richest ground on the server should be the most dangerous in every sense at once. And Wake players have no safe ground anywhere, which is the entire point of it.

The third clock — the Ledger

Heat recovers, which is what makes this a rotation instead of a death spiral. But some shafts get worked every day for months, and the world should remember that too.

The Ledger is the slow layer: a decaying float per region counting lifetime traffic, which never fully clears. High-Ledger shafts carry a permanently raised ambient Din floor — you walk in already at STIRRING, before you swing once, because this place has been loud for a year and something down here stopped waiting for a reason.

Past a threshold, something is living in it: a permanent named resident grown out of nothing but your server's own habits. Killable, worth killing, and worth arguing about whether to take it early or let it get fat first. Ship this last — it's the payoff, and it only means anything once your server has a year of habits to read.

Build order

  1. Din aloneExactly as planned. Tune it, live with it, break it, retune it for a month. Don't fuse anything into a system you haven't proven.
  2. Room heat as a numberOne float per region, deposited on break, decayed on a timer, four thresholds. No visuals, no seal, no payout — log it and watch where your server actually goes for two weeks.
  3. The WantingYour existing sound-driven mob AI with heat substituted for din as the target-selection input, plus a "don't disperse while warm" rule. Same code path. Reskin the models, write the Warmth, done.
  4. SteamMake it visible before you make it profitable. Players have to be able to read a room before you start paying them to gamble on one.
  5. The SealOne integer on the suit, three multipliers, a four-second field toggle with a spike on switch. Add it only once both factions exist — until then it's a dial with nothing on the other end.
  6. Payout multipliers, tuned low×2.5 at Warren is a guess. The real number is whatever makes a few people brave rather than everybody.
The failure mode to watch If room heat decays too slowly, your server cooks every good shaft in a fortnight and the map permanently degrades — new players arrive into a world that's already used up. Start decay generous at −200/hour and tighten only once you can see rotation actually happening. It is far easier to make a world harsher later than to explain why everywhere good is on fire.
